Two IKEAs
How IKEA's global model split under housing affordability pressure

By Pengcheng Lu & Samantha Wang

Act 1 · Origin
One global playbook

60 years, one playbook: suburban warehouse, flat-pack, self-assemble.

IKEA opened in Älmhult, Sweden in 1958. Families drove to a large store, chose affordable furniture, carried flat-packed boxes home, and assembled them themselves. For decades the model replicated unchanged — same big-box footprint across every continent IKEA entered.

Source: IKEA store locator and Wikipedia List of countries with IKEA stores.

Act 1 · Six decades, one map
Then the shape changed — but only in some regions

After 2018, city stores appear. After 2022, only East Asian ones dim out.

Watch the year advance on the map. North America and Europe fill with squares (big-box). After 2018 circles appear — city stores — and they stay open. East Asia gets city stores three years later (Shanghai Jing'an 2020, Tokyo Harajuku 2020, Shinjuku 2021) and most of them dim out in 2023 and 2026 as IKEA shuts them.

Source: IKEA Japan newsroom, Yicai Global, and Time Out Tokyo for the city-store opening and closure dates.

Act 2 · East Asia
High pressure, no city store

5 of the 8 highest-PTI East Asian cities: no city-format IKEA at all.

Taipei, Hong Kong, Beijing, Shenzhen and Guangzhou — five of the eight highest-PTI East Asian cities — never received a city-format IKEA. Where IKEA did try city format (Shanghai, Tokyo, Singapore), three of those six stores have already closed or are scheduled to.

Source: Numbeo Current Property Price-to-Income Ratio, the standard cross-city affordability proxy.

Act 2 · West
Same pressure, every store stayed open

Every Western city-format store opened since 2018: still open.

Same PTI scale, Western cities. London (Tottenham Court Rd, Hammersmith, Oxford Street), Paris (La Madeleine), NYC (Manhattan Studio), Vienna (Westbahnhof), San Francisco (Market Street) — every one of them is still operating.

Sources: IKEA UK, US, Austria and France newsroom press releases.

Act 3 · Western response
Physical format + service

Western response: smaller urban stores + service partnerships + AR app.

Service partnership is Western-only (TaskRabbit 2017). Urban format collects six dated openings between 2018 and 2025. The lone Western entry in channel innovation is the IKEA Place AR app (Sep 2017). The Buyback & Resell programme (2020) is global.

Sources: CNN Business, 9to5Mac (IKEA Place), IKEA UK / US / Austria newsrooms, Ingka Group.

Act 3 · East Asian response
Platforms + price + retreat

East response: 5 platform launches; the only regime with price cuts and closures.

Five East-Asian platform launches — Japan online 2017, Korea e-commerce 2018, China web 2019, Tmall flagship Mar 2020, JD.com flagship Aug 2025 — make East Asia the channel-innovation leader, contrary to the abstract's original framing. Two China price-cut events sit in 2024; five city-store closures sit on the bottom row with no Western equivalents.

Sources: Inside Retail Asia, The Investor, ChinaDaily, IKEA China JD.com flagship coverage, Yicai Global, Lifeweek.

Act 4 · East Asia
Why the East defends

East Asia glows warm: dense cities, weak DIY culture, strong local competition.

The matrix on the right shows IKEA running into structural headwinds in every East Asian market. The competitive consequence is concrete: Nitori posted ¥929 billion in FY2025 net sales (≈USD 6B, roughly half the Japanese furniture segment); Hanssem recorded ₩1.97 trillion in 2023 with about a 25% share of Korean interior furniture; meanwhile IKEA China FY24 revenue fell to RMB 11.15 billion — roughly 30% below its 2019 peak, and China has slipped from IKEA's 5th-largest market to its 10th.

Sources: Nitori IR; Statista on Hanssem; ConCall analysis of IKEA financials; Lifeweek and 21st Century Business Herald on China pricing.

Act 4 · West
Why the West expands

West glows cool: the conditions that made the original model work are still in place.

Same six dimensions, Western markets. Almost every cell sits in the blue range — strong DIY culture, high car ownership, low urban density, moderate service expectations, weak local manufacturing competition. The original model still fits, so the Western response can be additive (new city stores on top of the existing network) rather than defensive.

Coding anchored in Burt, Johansson & Thelander (2011); Burt, Dawson, Johansson & Hultman (2020); Ivarsson & Alvstam (2010).

Coda · Quantitative scorecard
Did the two responses actually work?

West: city stores 90% retained, revenue stable. East: half the city stores closed, China revenue −30%.

Coda · Playbook
Two playbooks, side by side

Same 8 priorities, near-mirrored ranking. The biggest flips: service partnerships (West #3 → East #8), urban-format (West #1 → East #5), channel innovation (East #1 → West #5).

Read the slope chart on the right against the scorecard you just saw. The Western playbook puts urban-format city stores, the big-box network, service partnerships and resale at the top — and the data shows that bet worked: 90% city-store retention, stable revenue. The East Asian playbook puts channel innovation, strategy repositioning, price defense and the big-box network at the top — and the data shows it is holding the line, not winning: half the city stores closed, China revenue down 30%, market position dropped five ranks.

Three takeaways. (1) Same global brand, same housing pressure, very different responses. (2) "West innovates on channel, East defends on price" is too clean — East Asia has actually led on both platform integration (Tmall, JD, Coupang) and formal strategy repositioning (FY24 revenue trough → RMB 6.3B reinvestment → Tokyo optimization). (3) The East-Asian retreat from city format (Jing'an 2023, Harajuku & Shinjuku 2026) is the part of the story that wasn't visible from outside until the data made it concrete.
