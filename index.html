One IKEA model, Two regional playbooks
How has housing affordability pressure shaped IKEA's strategy across Western and East Asian markets?

By Pengcheng Lu & Samantha Wang · ~10 min read

IKEA began with one global playbook: large suburban stores, flat-pack furniture, self-service, and affordability. Under housing affordability pressure, that model appears to adapt differently across regions: Western markets extend the original model through city-format stores and services, while East Asian markets shift toward platforms, price pressure, localization, and city-store retreat.

Act 2 · East Asia
High Pressure, Uneven City-Store Response

Read the left column — East Asian bars cluster at the top of the chart. Five of those high-pressure cities have no city-format IKEA, and two cities that did open one have since closed stores.

The chart shows both regions on a shared PTI scale. East Asian cities cluster at the top — Taipei, Hong Kong, Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, and Seoul all sit above 27. Yet the response on the left column is uneven: Taipei, Hong Kong, Beijing, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou have no city-format store at all; Shanghai opened one and later closed it; Tokyo opened one and has two more scheduled to close. Only Seoul and Singapore currently hold a single open city-format store each.

This pattern suggests that high-density, high-pressure markets may require more than a simple city-store adaptation — a question Act 3 picks up by looking at what IKEA did try in these markets.

Source: Numbeo Current Property Price-to-Income Ratio, the standard cross-city affordability proxy.

Act 2 · West
Housing Pressure, Stronger City-Store Retention

Read the right column — Western bars cluster in the lower half of the same chart. Yet six of these cities still operate at least one city-format store.

The chart is unchanged; only the focus shifts. Western cities cluster between PTI 7 and 16 — a noticeably lower band than the East Asian column on the left. Yet the city-format response on the right column is more durable: London, Paris, New York City, Vienna, Hamburg, and San Francisco all currently operate at least one city-format store. London has one closure since opening; the other cities have kept theirs.

The same global IKEA model meets a different combination of pressure and ecology in the West, and the city-format outcome is more stable. Act 4 will unpack the ecology side; Act 3 first looks at the responses IKEA tried.

Sources: IKEA UK, US, Austria, and France newsroom press releases.

Act 3 · East Asian Response
Platforms, Price, and Retreat

East Asian response: platform launches (Tmall, JD.com, Japan / Korea online), price cuts, and city-store closures.

The timeline shows that East Asia is not simply a "defensive" region. Platform and channel moves appear across Japan, Korea, and China.

But these innovations appear alongside price cuts, strategy repositioning, and city-store closures. Together, they suggest a platform-heavy playbook shaped by price pressure and city-store retreat.

This timeline organizes IKEA actions by year and response type, allowing readers to compare when different strategies appeared. Sources: Inside Retail Asia, The Investor, China Daily, IKEA China JD.com flagship coverage, Yicai Global, Lifeweek.

Act 3 · Western Response
Physical Format and Service

Western response: urban-format stores (London, Paris, NYC, Vienna), service partnerships (TaskRabbit), and add-on digital tools (Place AR app).

The timeline shows a mostly additive Western playbook. Urban-format stores, TaskRabbit, and IKEA Place add convenience around the original store model rather than replacing it.

Global programs such as Buyback & Resell support this broader adaptation pattern, but they are not Western-only.

Act 4 · East Asia
Why East Asia Faces Defensive Pressure

Higher pressure, tougher ecology: East Asia's denser cities and stronger local rivals make the original model harder to translate.

The matrix explains why East Asian strategy looks more pressured. Density is the most comparable metric, while service expectations and local competition are coded from documented market evidence.

Across the selected East Asian markets, higher density and stronger local competition increase the challenge to IKEA's original big-box, self-service model. This helps explain why the regional response shifts toward platforms, price moves, localization, and city-store retreat.

Act 4 · West
Why Western Markets Still Fit the Original Model

Lower pressure, gentler ecology: lower density and weaker local competition help the original model remain more viable.

The matrix shows why Western markets can build more easily on IKEA's original model. In the selected markets, density and local competition create lower overall challenge than in the East Asian comparison.

These conditions help explain why the Western response can add city-format stores and services on top of the existing big-box network, rather than shifting toward a more defensive playbook.

Methodology

The article draws on five datasets we compiled for this project: 57 IKEA stores with opening and closure years, 38 dated strategic events between 2016 and 2026, 24 cities with Numbeo price-to-income ratios as a housing affordability proxy, 10 markets coded across four retail-ecology dimensions (density, service expectations, local competition, DIY culture), and 18 quantitative external anchors (revenue figures, market shares, investment commitments) used to inform the playbook ranking. Density is sourced from national statistical agencies; competition and service signals are anchored where market-share data exists and labelled as observation elsewhere. The Coda playbook ranks are synthesized from dated events plus the external anchors using time decay (τ = 6y), parsed USD-equivalent magnitudes, and min-max normalization within each region. Full per-cell provenance is documented in the project's data audit.

Limitations

The store dataset is a representative sample, not the complete IKEA network; the playbook intensities reflect the events and anchors we were able to verify, not every internal IKEA decision. DIY culture and service expectation have no published country-level metric — we describe them as observational, not measured. The relationship between housing pressure and IKEA strategy is presented as a pattern observed alongside the data, not as a causal claim.
