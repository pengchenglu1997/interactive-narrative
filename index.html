Two IKEAs
How IKEA's global model split under housing affordability pressure

By Pengcheng Lu & Samantha Wang

Act 1
One IKEA, one model

In 1958, when Ingvar Kamprad opened the first IKEA store in Älmhult, Sweden, the bet was simple: families would drive to a large store, walk through a showroom, choose affordable furniture, carry flat-packed boxes home, and assemble them themselves. For decades, that model made IKEA one of the most recognizable furniture brands in the world.

Reading the map

The map on the right walks year by year through six decades of IKEA expansion. Until the late 2000s, almost every dot is a big-box suburban store. From 2018 onward, a new shape appears in city centres — circles for city stores, diamonds for planning studios, triangles for plan-&-order points. The new urban-format dots cluster very differently between Western and East Asian cities — and after 2022 some of the East Asian dots start dimming as those stores close.

Act 2
The housing shift

The conditions around home changed. In many major cities, young adults became more likely to rent, move frequently, live in smaller spaces, and delay homeownership. The 2024–25 English Housing Survey reports the average age of a first-time buyer in England at 34, and 35 in London (UK Government, 2025). In the U.S., McKinsey's October 2025 report finds the housing shortage nearly doubled between 2012 and 2023 to 8.2 million units, with rents up 30% versus wages up 20% from 2019 to 2023 (McKinsey, 2025).

Same pressure, different responses

The chart on the right ranks 24 cities by Numbeo's Price-to-Income Ratio (left bars) and overlays IKEA's actual city-format response (right markers, by year). Five of the eight highest-pressure East Asian cities — Taipei, Hong Kong, Beijing, Shenzhen, Guangzhou — never received a city-format IKEA at all (only big-box, shown as faint dots). Where East Asian IKEA did try city format (Shanghai Jing'an, Tokyo Harajuku, Tokyo Shinjuku), most of those stores closed in 2023–2026. Western high-PTI cities (London, Paris, NYC, SF, Vienna) all got city stores — and kept them.

Act 3
Two parallel adaptations

Both IKEAs are adapting. The shape of each adaptation differs sharply. In Western markets, IKEA's response is mostly physical-format and customer-service: smaller urban stores, the TaskRabbit acquisition (2017), the IKEA Place AR app (2017), and the global Buyback & Resell program (2020). In East Asian markets, the response is mostly platform integration plus price defense: IKEA Japan online shop (2017), IKEA Korea e-commerce (2018), Tmall flagship (Mar 2020), JD.com flagship (Aug 2025), a global EUR 2.1B price-cut investment (FY24), then a string of city-store closures.

Where the contrast is clearest

On the timeline at right, the service partnership row is essentially Western-only. The channel innovation row is mostly East-Asian: IKEA Place AR (2017, Western) is the lone Western entry against five East-Asian platform launches (Japan online 2017, Korea e-commerce 2018, China web 2019, Tmall 2020, JD 2025). The price cut and closure rows are East-only. The urban-format row is the most balanced (8 Western + 8 East-Asian tiles) — and the ground on which the two strategies meet and diverge.

Act 4
Why East Asia is different

Two reasons. Structural conditions — denser cities, weaker car culture, stronger service expectations, mature local manufacturing — make IKEA's original suburban big-box DIY model a harder fit. Competitive pressure — local champions operating at multiples of IKEA's local scale — has eroded IKEA's price advantage.

Coda
Two playbooks, side by side

If both IKEAs were ranking the same eight strategic priorities, they would rank them in almost opposite order. The slope chart connects each item across the two columns; steep lines are the rank flips. Three takeaways: (1) same global brand, same housing pressure, very different responses; (2) the original "West innovates on channel, East defends on price" framing is too clean — East Asia has actually led on platform integration while doing the deeper price cuts; (3) the East-Asian retreat from city format (Jing'an 2023, Harajuku & Shinjuku 2026) is the part of the story that wasn't visible from outside until the data made it concrete.
