One IKEA, Two Regional Playbooks
How IKEA Adapts to Housing Affordability Pressure Across Western and East Asian Markets

By Pengcheng Lu & Samantha Wang

Act 1 · Origin
One global playbook

60 years, one playbook: suburban warehouse, flat-pack, self-assemble.

IKEA opened in Älmhult, Sweden in 1958. For decades, its model largely replicated across regions: large suburban stores, flat-pack furniture, self-service, and affordability. This map establishes the original global playbook before the article compares how the model adapts across regions.

Source: IKEA store locator and Wikipedia List of countries with IKEA stores.

Act 1 · Six decades, one map
Then the shape changed — but only in some regions

After 2018, city stores appear. After 2022, several East Asian city-store markers begin to dim out.

As the year advances, the map shows IKEA's footprint expanding through big-box stores, then adding city-format stores after 2018. In Western markets, most city-format stores remain visible in the dataset. In East Asian markets, city-format stores appear later, including Shanghai Jing'an, Tokyo Harajuku, and Shinjuku, but several later dim out as stores close or are scheduled to close.

The 2018 shift wasn't aesthetic. In major cities, housing had become unaffordable enough that IKEA's typical young, renting customer was now downtown without a car — and the original "drive to a warehouse" model fit worse. The next two sections look at where that pressure was highest, and how IKEA responded.

Source: IKEA Japan newsroom, Yicai Global, and Time Out Tokyo for the city-store opening and closure dates.

Act 2 · East Asia
High pressure, uneven city-store response

Where housing pressure is highest, the old model fits worst. Yet five of the eight top-PTI East Asian cities have no city store.

Taipei, Hong Kong, Beijing, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou rank among the highest-PTI East Asian cities, yet they have no city-format IKEA. IKEA did test city formats in Shanghai, Tokyo, and Singapore, but several of those stores later closed or are scheduled to close. This pattern suggests that high-density, high-pressure markets may require more than a simple city-store adaptation.

Source: Numbeo Current Property Price-to-Income Ratio, the standard cross-city affordability proxy.

Act 2 · West
Housing pressure, stronger city-store retention

Same pressure, opposite result: most Western city stores opened since 2018 are still open.

Using the same PTI scale, Western cities also show housing affordability pressure. But compared with East Asian markets, the city-format response appears more durable: most Western city-format stores opened since 2018 remain in operation. This suggests that IKEA's urban format may fit more smoothly within these market conditions.

Sources: IKEA UK, US, Austria and France newsroom press releases.

Act 3 · East Asian response
Platforms + price + retreat

East Asian response: platform launches appear alongside price cuts and city-store retreat.

East Asia complicates a simple "West innovates, East defends" story. The region shows strong platform and channel innovation, including online launches and marketplace partnerships. At the same time, these moves appear alongside price cuts, strategy repositioning, and city-store closures, suggesting a platform-heavy playbook shaped by price pressure and city-store retreat.

This timeline organizes IKEA actions by year and response type, allowing readers to compare when different strategies appeared. Sources: Inside Retail Asia, The Investor, ChinaDaily, IKEA China JD.com flagship coverage, Yicai Global, Lifeweek.

Act 3 · Western response
Physical format + service

Western response: urban-format stores, service partnerships, and add-on digital tools.

In our dataset, Western markets show more visible activity around physical format adaptation and service partnerships. Urban stores, the TaskRabbit acquisition (2017, reported at around USD 42M but no exact figure publicly disclosed by IKEA), IKEA Place, and global programs such as Buyback & Resell suggest a strategy that adds convenience and services around the original store model rather than replacing it.

Sources: CNN Business, 9to5Mac (IKEA Place), IKEA UK / US / Austria newsrooms, Ingka Group.

Act 4 · East Asia
Why East Asia Faces Defensive Pressure

Same pressure, different ecology: East Asia's denser cities and stronger local rivals make the original model harder to translate.

The retail ecology matrix suggests that IKEA faces stronger structural headwinds across several East Asian markets. Urban density is the dimension we can cite directly: Tokyo's 23 wards average about 15,000 people per km², Seoul ~16,000, and Hong Kong / Singapore are denser still. The matrix also tracks service-bundling norms and local competition as observation-based dimensions (per-cell sources documented separately).

The business context reinforces this pressure: strong regional competitors such as Nitori and Hanssem, combined with IKEA China's reported revenue decline from its 2019 peak, suggest that East Asian markets require more localized adaptation rather than a simple replication of the original model. The consumer-behavior side of the story — including the documented lower willingness to self-assemble furniture — is described in Burt, Dawson, Johansson & Hultman (2020) and stays in the text rather than in the matrix, because no comparable country-level metric for "DIY culture" exists.

Method note: density figures sourced from national statistical agencies (Tokyo Statistical Yearbook; Statistics Korea; Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department; Singapore Department of Statistics; National Bureau of Statistics of China). Competition signals anchored where market-share data exists (Statista on Hanssem ~25% Korean interior share; Nitori IR FY25 ~¥929B); labelled as observation across mid-size players elsewhere. Other sources: ConCall analysis of IKEA financials; Lifeweek and 21st Century Business Herald on China pricing.

Act 4 · West
Why the Original Model Still Fits Many Western Markets

Same pressure, different ecology: Western markets still fit the original model — density low, competition weak — so additions stick.

Compared with East Asian markets, many Western markets show conditions that better support IKEA's original model. Density is the dimension most directly citable: US Census and Statistics Canada data put US / Canada metropolitan density well below East Asian peers, with most IKEA store catchments calibrated to suburban / exurban densities of 500–1,500 people per km². Competition is also weaker by negative observation — no single dominant domestic furniture chain operates at IKEA's price-and-format scale in the US, UK, Germany, or Canada.

The consumer-side observation about stronger DIY culture in Western markets (Home Depot ~USD 157B FY24 + Lowe's ~USD 83B; OBI / Hornbach / Bauhaus all multi-billion-euro retailers in DACH) is documented here rather than scored in the matrix because no clean country-level DIY metric exists. The Western response builds on the existing big-box network through city stores and service additions, rather than shifting defensively.

Method note: density figures from US Census Bureau, UK Office for National Statistics, Eurostat, Statistics Canada, and INSEE. DIY-retail figures: Home Depot 10-K FY24, Kingfisher Annual Report, and OBI / Hornbach / Bauhaus annual disclosures. Anchored academically in Burt, Johansson & Thelander (2011); Burt, Dawson, Johansson & Hultman (2020); Ivarsson & Alvstam (2010).

Coda · Playbook
Two playbooks, side by side

Same brand, same housing pressure, different retail ecology: one IKEA model becomes two regional playbooks.

The slope chart compares the same eight strategic priorities across two regional playbooks. Price pressure appears in both regions, but the rest of the ranking shows different strategic emphasis.

On the Western side, the dominant signal is the global EUR 2.1B price commitment, then urban-format city stores and the maintained big-box network — a strategy that extends the original model with additions rather than replacing it. On the East Asian side, the dominant signal is also price defense, but it is paired with strategy repositioning (China #5 → #10, announced reinvestment, Tokyo Harajuku and Shinjuku closures) and local design integration (Nitori ¥929B, Hanssem ~25% of Korean interior) — a more locally adaptive playbook shaped by price pressure, platform shifts, and local competition.

The takeaway: one global IKEA model becomes two regional playbooks under different housing pressures and retail ecologies.
